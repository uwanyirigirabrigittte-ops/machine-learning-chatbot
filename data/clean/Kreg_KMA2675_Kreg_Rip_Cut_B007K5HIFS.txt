Rating: 4.0/5.0 | Date: 05 7, 2012 | Helpful votes: 91
Heading: Another Fine Tool Aid by Kreg
Having purchased and owned several Kreg products in the past, I was not hesitant to try the Rip Cut by Kreg. As a casual observer, I could tell that the intent of this product was to allow the user to adapt their own saw to the tool, cut straight rips through sheet products and most of all, do so without the use of a pencil or straight edge or even tape measure.With all Kreg products, you will find that the Rip Cut comes in a good package with all loose items secured in heavy bags and as usual, everything in it's place. The device assembles easily with a few screws, nuts and plastic / poly parts. The saw base is attached to the blue Kreg saw base using a pair of metal hold down clips with pointed set screws. The saw base then slides onto the straight edge and with the friction clamp, it holds your saw at your set distance with no problem.Using the Rip Cut is fast and easy. It's a good idea to readjust the red pointer every time you remount the saw to the base but the process is fast and simple. I just measured from the edge of the sheet product over 12" and marked it with a pencil. Place the saw on the mark (or off to the blade width side) with the edge guide firmly against the edge of the sheet and slide the red indicator to match up with the 12" mark on the straight edge. Tighten the screws on the red indicator and you are good to go. One nice thing is that if you want to make a rip, just slide the base along the straight edge, lock in your distance with the cam lock and cut away. If you want to make a cross cut or other cut that does not need the straight edge or guide, simply release the cam lock, slide the saw with the blue base attached all the way to the end and off of the straight edge and viola... you have your circular saw free from the Rip Cut so you can make all the other cuts you need. When it's time to make another sheet rip, simply slide it in place, lock it down and away you go. Very convenient.I was able to cut a couple dozen sheets of 4 x 8 goods down to 2 x 8 with no measuring or marking. Simply slide the guide along the outer edge of your sheet goods and in a matter of about 10 seconds, you have an 8' long cut that is as straight and accurate as any factory sheet edge. The Rip Cut is very light and yet strong and durable. I found it to be well worth the 34 dollars I spent on it at the local Lowes big box store.I did put some Johnson's Paste Wax on all surfaces that will contact other surfaces prior to the first use. I can't tell if there is a difference but I can say that the saw, guide and guide bar slid like magic. There is one thing that is not "bad" but just something to pay attention to. The Blue saw base tends to droop a bit as it has no way of attaching to the back of the saw base itself. It is not a show stopper by any means but it just give the saw / guide as a whole a bit of a loose feeling. Truly it's just a mental thing more than anything else.I would gladly recommend this to a friend or family member if they find that the awkward handling of full size sheet goods on a table saw is uncomfortable or if they use clamped straight edges to get that fast rip cut on several pieces of sheet goods. Use it 6 times a year and you'll be thrilled you got one... use it just 2 times a year and well... maybe you'd do better spending that money on clamps or other common place tools for the shop.Here's to cutt'n up...Larry

Heading: Better than most, but far from perfect
Compared to those overpriced plastic T-squares that pass as rip guides, this thing is a lot better in many ways--it's more accurate, a whole lot sturdier, and it works no matter which direction your blade faces.Assembly is surprisingly complicated. It shouldn't be beyond you if you do a lot of building, but I have absolutely no idea why the little plastic clamping thing with all the little parts couldn't be pre-assembled in the package.Attaching it to the saw involves driving a couple of pointed set screws into the metal plate on the saw itself. Depending on where your saw's angle adjustment levers fall, tightening these down in a suitable location may be very, very difficult, and it's never going to be super secure on the sled as there's really nothing more than friction holding it in place. The more you tighten, the more the attachment brackets want to lift up out of the plastic, and it's not difficult to imagine them busting right out of it, ruining the entire thing.Actually making cuts is a two-handed affair--one to push the saw and the other to make sure the guide bar runs flush against the edge of the board throughout. If you're making a very narrow cut, this will place one hand very, very, very close to the blade. Be extremely careful.If you're cutting large sheets of things the way they do in the demo videos, it's pretty easy to clamp them down and make your cuts. If you're ripping narrower boards, you're going to find that there is absolutely no way that you can clamp them down to anything and still get this guide to traverse the length of the cut--it's just too darn long and it will always hit the clamps. The only thing you can do is nail the board you're cutting to another board underneath it and sacrifice a quarter inch or so in the process. Not the end of the world for pine, but if you've got some expensive hardwood you're looking to make the most of, you really need something more expensive than this aftermarket attachment. I'm contemplating taking an angle grinder to mine and making it a whole lot shorter as I rarely need to make rip cuts wider than four inches.It absolutely will not take the place of a real rail-guided saw, but it's OK for what they're charging.

Heading: Requires a bit of caution when setting it up.... This is definitely not a replacement for a decent table saw.- It'll help accurately rip a sheet of plywood, but if the width of the piece being cut is less than 24", the aluminum rail will run into the clamp that's used to hold the plywood in place.- Only two set screws hold the saw base plate in position on the blue plastic plate. And that's assuming that the front of your saw baseplate is perpendicular to the saw blade itself.If you're careful when attaching the saw to the blue sled, this works really well...I'm using it to cut the sheet goods for some cabinets and it's preforming without a hitch. Heading: Much easier than using a standard tablesaw on 4x8 sheets
This is a great replacement for the standard guide that you get with a circular saw. The ability to rip out to 24" easily is a great thing, as it is very difficult to handle a 4'x8' sheet of 3/4" plywood by yourself when trying to run it on a tablesaw. This will solve a lot of problems for you and you will be very glad that you have it once you understand a couple of simple tricks.1. Put your workpiece on top of a sacrificial piece that is large enough to hold your entire workpiece and stop your cut-off piece from falling. This will keep your saw from binding and it will keep your cut straight. Just adjust the cutting depth of your circular saw to the minimum depth to get through your workpiece fully and use your sacrificial board to hold your workpiece for you.2. Mind your cord. Make sure that you keep your cord out of the way so that you don't accidentally step on it, or get it bound in some way. This simple step will save you a lot of heartache in any job, so pay particular attention.3. Keep your workpiece clean and free of debris. The guide will slide much better if you keep your workpiece free of excess sawdust and also keep the slide clean and smooth.One other thing to note: the picture of the guide shows it configured for using your saw with your left-hand. You can assemble the guide so that the saw is used with the right hand, all you need to do is put the pieces on the other side. As shown in the picture, the guide rides down the right side of your workpiece and you must use your saw with your left hand, just change the position of the plastic pieces and the guide will ride down the left side of your workpiece for a right-handed worker.If I could change one thing about this jig, I would make the gauge for the ruler transparent with a line down the middle. As it stands, the gauge comes to a point and you point the tip to your length. This should be a simple transparent prism with a line like most fences. I am considering making one of my own, as it would make it much easier to be precise.Bottom line: Highly recommended5/5 stars

Heading: Affordable intermediate solution
I have been using Kreg pocket hole jigs and accessories for a number of years, and have always enjoyed much success.But as their success grew, I noticed a larger variety of the ubiquitous blue devices showing up in catalogs and stores.None really attracted my attention until I saw the Rip Cut. Being right in the middle of turning 5' X 5' squares of Baltic Birch into speaker cabinets, I was a little bit weary of either using my 8' long shooting board for repeated cuts as small as 10' wide, or trusting myself to follow a line to perfection freehand, or trying to manhandle the blanks onto my poor ol' Ryobi 10" table saw, which was never, ever intended for production work. I have to fine tune the rip fence with a deadblow hammer and a machinist's rule if I want anything approaching a square and true edge.The Rip Cut solves this problem and a few more, but not without creating a few new problems of its own. I found on a large enough blank it made consistent and nicely square cuts. Smaller pieces become problematic, the thing is so (relatively) large you suddenly have no way to clamp the workpiece without the 24" aluminum rule hitting the clamp dead on. My solution- for now- was to clamp the work well back of the edge, stop the saw when it got there, then re-clamp behind it. Crude, I know, but it was the best I could do for now. And it worked. A better solution may be re-thinking how one goes about laying out cuts. I suspect it would involve more waste, but not as much as a few botched cuts on the table saw.As with any new device involving relative accuracy, I refused to take its scale seriously for finishing cuts. I know with time this may change, but for now I have to hedge my bet. One large oversight is the adjustment arrow which follows your saw to the correct dimension. Not only is the tip plastic and slightly rounded over, it is red plastic and completely covers up the scale below. All the fractional marks look - unmarked. You almost have to look at, say, 6-3/8" and put a pencil dot on t so you can be sure to hit the mark. Simply changing to a sharply pointed clear marker would be an effortless fix for them, and unless it's just me, I hope others voice a similar opinion.A larger issue at this time seems to be the last " of any cut. As you need to hold the guide with one hand firmly against the work, and steer the saw with the other, when you reach the end of the road suddenly you discover you do not have enough hands, and there is so much of that lovely blue plastic locking things together it is even difficult to freehand to the edge. A lot of roller stand support below deck helps, but hanging on to everything without ruining the last inch of the cut is definitely a new skill to be acquired. As it was, I ended up with 2 2" long tearouts when the drop separated. Realizing there would be a learning curve including stuff like this, I gave the blanks generous oversizing to accommodate. I find the Rip Cut makes a nice enough edge my Ryobi is quite content to accurately trim to spec. Since I cannot at this time acquire the "real" tools that would make this "DIY" device unnecessary, it is a great, reasonably priced interim solution.

Heading: Accurate and easy to use, but a little fussy, and perhaps a bit fragile. I bought my Rip-Cut based on experience with Kreg's pocket hole jogs (I own two), positive reviews here, and the fact that the local Rockler store had it on sale for twenty-nine bucks. Not bad. It went together quickly, and setting it up to my saw- I decided to use it with my old Porter-Cable 314 Trim Saw- was straightforward. It can be a little fussy, as calibration requires adjusting the saw so the blade just "kisses" the rip guide, and then adjusting the pointer to zero. O n a small saw like the 314, the blade can and will hit the mounting plate, something I dealt with by cutting out a 1/4" square piece from the plate.Once it's assembled and calibrated it's very easy to use, and with a smooth running saw like the 314 and a plywood blade you can get excellent results. If you remove the saw and remount it later, you have to go though the setup and calibration process again; I would like to to see Kreg add some kind of locating jig in the mounting plate that would speed up this process. The other issue I have is that this jig is largely made of plastic. While this does keep the price down, I think an all-metal version would be well worth the $100 or whatever it would cost.There's also a limitation I didn't notice until I'd used it a few times: The track is 24" long, and that means you need 24" of room clear of clamps on the sheet you'r cutting, even if you're only taking a small slice. Otherwise the clamp will interfere with the movement of the jig as it slides along the sheet. If you have a half or quarter sheet, 24" wide, you can't easily use this jig. You either need to cut without clamping the sheet to a support (not recommended) or cut part way, move the clamps, cut again, move, etc.The big question for me is this: Is this jig substantially better than the jig I usually use, which is simply a long piece of tempered hardboard glued and screwed to a piece of 1/2" ply? The Kreg is more compact to store, but it's not really any more accurate, and it takes a lot more time to set up. I'll have to wait and see which gets more use in the coming year.
